20-Second Explanation

What Is Gum Disease?

Gum disease is inflammation and infection affecting the tissues that surround and support your teeth. Its early form, gingivitis, mainly affects the gums. Periodontitis involves deeper supporting tissues and can lead to loss of bone around the teeth.

Common Gum Disease Symptoms

What Are You Noticing?

You do not need to know whether you have gingivitis or periodontitis before booking. Start with what you are seeing, feeling or repeatedly noticing.

01

My gums bleed when I brush

Repeated bleeding can be a sign of inflammation, even when there is little or no pain.

02

My gums look red or swollen

Gums that stay puffy, tender or visibly inflamed deserve attention rather than being dismissed as temporary irritation.

03

My bad breath keeps coming back

Persistent odor can have several causes, including plaque and bacteria around the teeth and gums.

04

My teeth look longer

This can happen when the gumline recedes and more of the tooth or root becomes visible.

05

An adult tooth feels loose

Mobility should be evaluated promptly because loss of support around a tooth can be one possible reason.

06

My bite feels different

Changes in the tissues supporting the teeth can sometimes allow teeth to shift or feel different when you bite.

Early vs. Advanced Periodontal Disease

Gingivitis vs. Periodontitis: What's the Difference?

Gingivitis and periodontitis are related forms of gum disease, but they affect the tissues supporting your teeth differently. Understanding the difference helps explain why the stage matters.

Plaque around the gumline

When plaque is not removed consistently, bacteria can irritate gum tissue and contribute to inflammation.

Hardened tartar

Once plaque mineralizes into calculus, brushing and flossing cannot remove it. Professional cleaning is needed.

Smoking or tobacco use

Smoking is an important periodontal risk factor and can also make periodontal treatment less successful.

Diabetes, medications & genetics

Medical conditions, certain medications, hormonal changes and individual susceptibility can influence periodontal risk.

Home Care vs. Professional Care

Can I Treat Gum Disease at Home?

Good home care matters, especially in prevention and early inflammation, but it has clear limits once tartar or deeper periodontal changes are present.

What you can do every day

  • Brush twice daily with fluoride toothpaste using gentle technique.
  • Clean between teeth daily using floss or another interdental device appropriate for your mouth.
  • Clean your tongue and stay hydrated.
  • Avoid tobacco and ask your health professional for support if you want to quit.

What home care cannot do

  • It cannot remove hardened tartar once it has formed.
  • It cannot tell how much supporting bone may have been affected.
  • Mouthwash alone cannot restore periodontal tissue that has already been lost.
  • Persistent bleeding, mobility, pus or swelling should not be managed by guessing at home.

Common Questions

Questions Patients Ask About Gum Disease

Why do my gums bleed when I brush or floss?

Bleeding can occur when gum tissue is inflamed, although irritation from aggressive brushing or other causes is also possible. Persistent bleeding deserves evaluation.

Can gum disease go away on its own?

Early gingivitis can improve when plaque and tartar are professionally managed and home care improves. Periodontitis involves deeper tissue changes and requires professional management.

Does gum disease always hurt?

No. Gum disease can develop with little or no pain, which is why bleeding, recession, pocket measurements and other findings matter.

Is persistent bad breath a sign of gum disease?

It can be associated with gum disease, but bad breath can have several other causes. A dental evaluation can help determine whether the teeth or gums are contributing.

Can receding gums grow back?

Lost gum tissue generally does not simply grow back on its own. Depending on the cause and severity, a dentist may recommend monitoring, changing contributing habits or considering periodontal treatment.

Can I get dental implants if I've had gum disease?

A history of gum disease does not automatically rule out implants, but active disease and available bone support need to be evaluated before implant treatment is planned.
